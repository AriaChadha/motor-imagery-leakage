EEG-Based Wheelchair Control Across Healthy and Post-Stroke Populations

Does adaptive signal amplification improve motor imagery decoding?

August 13, 2026

Abstract

Motor imagery brain-computer interfaces decode movement intent from scalp EEG, but signal quality is reduced after stroke, which limits decoding accuracy in exactly the population that stands to gain most from wheelchair control. This work tests whether adaptive per-trial signal amplification, scaling low-amplitude trials toward a healthy reference, recovers any of that lost accuracy. Two public datasets were used: PhysioNet EEGMMIDB for healthy controls and the figshare 21679035 corpus for post-stroke patients, harmonized to 29 shared channels at 160 Hz over a 4 s motor imagery window. Random Forest and EEGNet classifiers were evaluated both within subject and across subjects, and decoding accuracy drove a discrete-event navigation simulation. Statistical comparison was not available for this run. For the healthy group, per-user calibration reached 0.623 while transfer to an unseen subject reached 0.598, a difference of +2.5 percentage points. For the stroke group, per-user calibration reached 0.541 while transfer to an unseen subject reached 0.501, a difference of +4.0 percentage points. Uniform per-trial scaling multiplies signal and noise together and leaves every within-trial ratio unchanged, so the signal-to-noise ratio available to the classifier is unaltered while genuine between-trial amplitude variation is compressed. The result is reported as a negative finding, and it constrains the design of future amplification schemes: a useful method has to act selectively on frequency bands or spatial components rather than scaling the trial as a whole.

1. Methods

1.1 Datasets

Dataset Group Paradigm Native rate
PhysioNet EEGMMIDB, runs 4, 8, 12 Healthy controls Left vs right fist motor imagery 160 Hz
figshare 21679035 (Liu et al. 2024) Post-stroke Left vs right hand motor imagery 500 Hz

Both are reduced to the 29 channels the two montages share, resampled to 160 Hz, band-pass filtered from 1 to 40 Hz, and cut to a 4 s imagery window. Trials with any channel exceeding 150 µV peak-to-peak are rejected, using the same threshold for both groups.

1.2 Re-referencing

The datasets do not share a reference. The stroke recordings are referenced to CPz, which suppresses amplitude at electrodes near the vertex: median peak-to-peak at CZ was 7.6 times lower than in the healthy group and 6.7 times lower at PZ, while peripheral sites such as T8 and TP8 differed by only about two times. That gradient is centred on the reference electrode rather than on sensorimotor cortex, so it reflects the montage and not stroke physiology. Both groups are therefore re-referenced to their own common average before any amplitude comparison is made. Without this step the amplification factor, derived from a healthy-versus-stroke RMS comparison, would largely measure the difference in reference scheme between two laboratories.

1.2 Amplification

deficit = (baseline_rms - trial_rms) / baseline_rms
factor  = clip(1.0 + deficit * 0.5, AMP_MIN, AMP_MAX)

The baseline is the mean trial RMS of the healthy group. Only trials below that baseline are scaled, and labels are never modified.

1.3 Evaluation

Two protocols are reported throughout. Within-subject accuracy uses stratified cross-validation inside each subject and averages across subjects, matching a system calibrated for one user. Cross-subject accuracy uses GroupKFold with subject as the group, so no subject appears in both train and test, and estimates transfer to a new user. Trials are never pooled across subjects into an ungrouped split, which would place the same subject on both sides of the fold and inflate accuracy.

3. Limitations

The navigation results come from a simulation, not a physical wheelchair. The simulated environment is a 10x10 grid with static obstacles and a fixed goal, which omits dynamic obstacles, continuous control, and the physical dynamics of a real chair, so the absolute navigation numbers should be read as a comparison between conditions rather than as a prediction of real-world performance.

The healthy and stroke recordings come from different laboratories and different amplifiers. An amplitude difference between the groups survives common average referencing, and because the recordings are instrumentally distinct that residual gap is more plausibly a matter of amplifier gain than of physiology. It propagates directly into the amplification factor. When nearly every impaired trial falls below the baseline, the adaptive rule degenerates into a near-uniform gain and is no longer adapting to anything, which weakens the interpretation of the amplification condition. Computing the baseline within each dataset rather than across them would address this and is the natural next experiment.

Within-subject models train on far fewer trials than cross-subject models, which can place the within-subject estimate below the cross-subject estimate when trials per subject are limited. The two numbers answer different questions and their magnitudes are not directly comparable.

CSP is fitted on all trials before cross-validation rather than inside each fold, which biases the CSP contribution upward. Both phases are affected identically, so the Phase 1 to Phase 2 comparison is unaffected, but absolute accuracies are optimistic.
